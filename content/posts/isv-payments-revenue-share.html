If you run a vertical software company, sooner or later a payments partner will hand you a revenue share offer. One says "60% of net." Another says "25 basis points." A third says "up to 80%." They all sound good, and they're almost impossible to compare at a glance.

This guide walks through how ISV payments revenue share is actually calculated, what gets subtracted before you see a dollar, and the contract terms that matter more than the headline number.

The two ways revenue share gets quoted

Almost every offer is one of these:

  • Basis points on gross volume. You earn a fixed slice of everything your merchants process. 25 bps on $1 million is $2,500. Simple, predictable and easy to check.
  • A percentage of net revenue. You earn a share of the margin left after costs. This is the classic "residual split," and it's where most of the confusion lives, because "net" depends on what gets taken out first.

Neither is better by default. A net split can pay far more on well-priced merchants. Basis points on gross can be safer if you expect pricing pressure. You have to model both on your own numbers.

How a net residual is calculated

Start with what the merchant pays. On interchange-plus pricing, that's the card brands' costs (interchange, plus dues and assessments) passed straight through, plus a markup, say 0.50% and $0.10 per transaction.

Interchange is not revenue for anyone in your deal. It goes to the card-issuing banks, so it drops out of the math entirely. What's left is the markup.

From the markup, the processor subtracts its own cost to serve you. That cost is your buy rate, usually listed on a document called a Schedule A: a small percentage, a per-transaction fee and often a monthly fee per merchant. What remains is net revenue, and that's what gets split.

$5,000,000 a month in volume, 50,000 transactions

Markup: 0.50% + $0.10 = $25,000 + $5,000 = $30,000
Buy rate: 0.10% + $0.05 = $5,000 + $2,500 = $7,500
Net revenue = $22,500

60% of net = $13,500 a month
30% of net = $6,750 a month
25 bps on gross = $12,500 a month

Same volume, three offers, and the gap between them is almost $7,000 a month. Notice too that the 60% offer only wins because the merchants carry a healthy markup. Cut the markup in half to win a competitive deal and that split pays a lot less, while basis points on gross wouldn't move.

What gets subtracted before the split

The buy rate is the obvious cost. The less obvious ones are where offers quietly get worse. Ask for a full Schedule A and look for:

  • Per-merchant monthly fees for things like gateway access, statements, PCI programs or account maintenance.
  • Batch, payout and ACH fees charged every time a merchant gets funded.
  • Chargeback and retrieval fees. Find out whether they come out of your residual or get billed to the merchant.
  • Merchant losses. If a merchant runs up chargebacks and disappears, some agreements make the software partner share the loss. That's a big deal if you serve higher-risk industries.
  • Monthly minimums on your total residual or per merchant, below which you're paid nothing.

A 70% split with five deductions can easily pay less than a 50% split with none.

The contract terms that matter more than the split

The percentage gets the attention. These terms decide whether the income actually lasts:

  • Who sets merchant pricing. If the processor can lower a merchant's rate to keep them, your net residual shrinks without your say.
  • Ownership and portability. If you part ways, do your residuals keep paying on merchants you already boarded? Can you move those merchants? Get it in writing.
  • Vesting and termination. Some agreements stop residuals if you're terminated, or only vest them after a set period.
  • Reporting. You want merchant-level reports every month, with volume, transactions, pricing and costs, so you can check the math yourself.
  • Tiers. If the split rises with volume, know the thresholds and whether the higher rate applies to all volume or only the volume above the line.
  • Term and exit. Contract length, auto-renewal and early termination costs.

What actually grows the number

Negotiating the split matters, but adoption usually matters more. A great revenue share on 15% of your merchants loses to an average one on 60%. That comes from making payments the easy default inside your product, which we cover in how SaaS companies make money from embedded payments.

The other lever is keeping merchants approved. Every merchant your processor declines or shuts down takes its residual with it. Adding a second approval path for those merchants turns lost volume back into revenue without touching the ones who are doing fine.

A quick checklist for any offer

  • Is the share on gross volume or net revenue, and how is "net" defined?
  • Can I see the full Schedule A, every fee included?
  • Who pays for chargebacks and merchant losses?
  • Who controls merchant pricing?
  • What happens to my residuals if we part ways?
  • Will I get merchant-level reporting every month?

If a partner can answer all six clearly, you can compare offers on equal footing. If they can't, that tells you something too.
