What is a rolling reserve, and how do you get it lowered?

A rolling reserve holds back part of every deposit for months. Here's how it works, what it does to cash flow, and how to get it lowered or removed.

By Aaron Puttmann, TrxnPop

You got approved for a merchant account. Then your first deposits came in a little light, and somewhere in the agreement you find the words "rolling reserve." The processor is keeping part of your money, and you want to know how much, for how long and how to make it stop.

Here's how reserves work, what one really does to your cash flow, and what tends to get one reduced.

What a rolling reserve is

A rolling reserve is a percentage of each day's card sales that your processor holds back for a set period, then releases to you. A common setup is something like 5% to 10% held for 90 to 180 days, though the exact terms vary a lot by processor, industry and history.

The money is still yours. It isn't a fee. It's a cushion the processor keeps in case your business generates chargebacks, refunds or losses it can't collect from you later.

Why would they need that? When a customer disputes a charge, the processor pays the card network first and then pulls the money back from you. If your account is empty, or you've closed up shop, the processor eats the loss. Card dispute windows commonly run around 120 days from the transaction, and longer for some situations like future-dated services, so processors want a cushion that lasts at least that long.

How the math works

Say you process $50,000 a month and your terms are a 10% rolling reserve held for 180 days.

$50,000 × 10% = $5,000 held each month

Months 1–6: $5,000 held, $0 released
Month 7 onward: $5,000 held, $5,000 released

Peak balance in reserve: $5,000 × 6 = $30,000

For the first six months, your deposits are 10% lighter than your sales. After that, the oldest money starts coming back each month at the same pace new money goes in, so your deposits return to roughly normal. But about $30,000 of your money stays parked with the processor for as long as the reserve is in place.

That first six months is where businesses get squeezed. If your margins are thin or you're growing fast, a 10% haircut on deposits can be the difference between making payroll comfortably and not.

Other types of reserves

  • Capped (or fixed) reserve. The processor holds a percentage of each deposit until the reserve hits a set dollar amount, then stops. The cap stays in place for the life of the account or until it's reviewed. This is often easier to live with, because once the cap is met your deposits go back to full.
  • Upfront reserve. You deposit a lump sum, or the processor holds a chunk of your first deposits, before you process at full speed.
  • Reserve after closure. When an account is terminated, the processor may hold remaining funds for months to cover late chargebacks. That's a different situation, and we cover it in what to do when a processor closes your account.

Why you got one

Underwriters add reserves when they see risk they can't price in through rates alone. The usual reasons:

  • Your industry. Bail bonds, travel, ticketing, subscriptions, CBD, firearms and credit repair all carry higher dispute or refund risk in the eyes of processors.
  • Future delivery. If customers pay today for something delivered weeks or months later, like trips, events or memberships, there's a long window for disputes.
  • Limited processing history. New businesses, or businesses new to cards, have no track record to show.
  • Past chargebacks or a closed account. Recent statements with high dispute ratios, or a prior termination, make underwriters cautious. If you were added to the MATCH list, read how to get off the MATCH list first.
  • Large average tickets or fast growth. A big ticket size or a projected volume much larger than your history increases the potential loss.

How to get a reserve lowered or removed

Reserves aren't always permanent. Many agreements include a review after a set period, and even when they don't, you can ask. What helps:

  1. Read your agreement first. Find the reserve percentage, hold period, any cap and any review date. Know what you agreed to before you ask for a change.
  2. Build a clean track record. Six months or more with low chargebacks and refunds is the strongest argument you have.
  3. Keep disputes down on purpose. Use a billing descriptor customers recognize, send clear receipts, make your refund policy easy to find and answer customer complaints before they turn into chargebacks.
  4. Ask for a capped reserve instead. If removal is off the table, converting a rolling reserve to a capped one often gets your deposits back to full sooner.
  5. Make the request in writing. Include your processing history, chargeback ratio and anything that's changed, like new policies or a longer operating history.

If your processor won't move after a long clean stretch, it may be worth seeing what another provider that specializes in your industry would offer. Your statements are your leverage.

Questions to ask before you sign

  • What percentage is held, and for how many days?
  • Is there a cap, and what is it?
  • When is the first review, and what do you need to see to lower it?
  • If the account closes, how long are funds held afterward?

A good provider will answer all four in plain English and put the answers in the agreement. If the terms are vague, that's a warning sign.

This post is general information about how card processing works, not legal or financial advice. Reserve terms differ by processor and agreement.

Quick answers

Is a rolling reserve a fee?

No. The money is still yours. The processor holds it for a set time and then releases it, so it hits cash flow, not your bottom line.

How long does a rolling reserve last?

Holds of 90 to 180 days are common, and many processors review the reserve after six to twelve months of clean processing. A low chargeback rate and steady volume are what usually get it lowered.

Can I negotiate a rolling reserve?

Often, yes. Asking for a capped reserve, a lower percentage or a scheduled review date are all reasonable, especially if you can show clean processing history.
